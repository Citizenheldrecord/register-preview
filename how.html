How it works

Every promise becomes a yes-or-no test with three parts.

  • An outcome. What must be true for the test to come out yes.
  • A date. The one day it is checked. The government's own date comes first, then a date set in law, and otherwise twelve months from the announcement.
  • An owner. The person or body who answers for it.

If any of the three is missing, the promise is not entered. The date is set when the promise is entered and is not moved later.

Scored on the day, from the official source

On the scoring date the test is checked against the official record, such as the Canada Gazette, a court docket, the consolidated statute or a government release. The result is passed (a PASS) or failed (a FAILED), with a link to the source. Passed means the dated test came out yes on its date. It is not the last word: the register keeps tracking what happens afterwards, and where delivery comes later, a follow-up promise with its own date tracks it.

When a promise fails

A failure is written up in a dated entry. It names where the promise got stuck, such as financing or a political choice, and what the failure cost where that can be sourced. The write-up is published within 30 days. Where the government sets a new date, that becomes a new promise with its own test. The failed line stays.

Hype, means and cost

Each result also gets three plain grades, filled in only after it is scored.

  • Hype. Did the announcement match what was delivered?
  • Means. Were the tools and money actually put in place?
  • Cost. What did it cost, compared with what was claimed?

Each is graded matched, mixed, failed or open. The grades never change the pass or fail.

Nothing is rewritten

A result is entered once. If it turns out to be wrong, the first line stays and a new dated line records the correction. Rows stay open until they are scored.

The full rules are on how the register works, and every column is explained in the register in plain English.
